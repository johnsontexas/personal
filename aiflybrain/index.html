What this is

A fruit fly's brain has been mapped synapse by synapse. The FlyWire connectome lists every neuron and every connection between them. Most AI runs on made-up networks. This one runs on a real animal's wiring diagram, and it is not a simulation of a fly doing fly things: it is a machine learning to read, remember and answer questions, using a fly's brain as its body.

The vessel is the fly's central brain: 35,953 neurons joined by 960,585 real connections carrying 14.5 million synapses. Training changes only what a brain can change — how strong each synapse is, and how each neuron rests, leaks and tires. Which neuron connects to which is never touched. That is the fly's, and it is fixed.

Everything on this page runs on your own device. There is no server and no API: your browser loads the wiring and the learned synapse strengths, then spikes the neurons itself.

How a letter becomes a thought

1. It smells the letterEvery character has its own odour: a fixed pattern over the fly's 106 olfactory glomeruli. Typing h releases the smell of h onto its receptor neurons.
2. Spikes cross the brainThe signal travels the real connectome for five ticks — long enough to cross the three or four synapses from nose to output.
3. Its mouth answersThe 1,299 descending neurons (the cables a real fly uses to command its legs and wings) are split into one group per character. Whichever group is driven hardest is the letter it says.
4. It remembersNothing is reset between letters. What it has read so far lives on as voltage and fatigue in its neurons — that is its short-term memory.

Honest notes

  • It is not conscious, and it is not a fly. It is a fly's wiring diagram used as a computer. A real fly never reads and has no idea what a letter is.
  • It is slow to teach. Every letter costs five ticks across 36k neurons and nearly a million synapses, forwards and then backwards to learn: about 1,000× slower than an ordinary AI model of the same size. Hours of training here are worth seconds of training there.
  • Only 5+ synapse connections are kept, FlyWire's usual confidence threshold, and signs follow Dale's law: a neuron that excites can never learn to inhibit.
  • The honest test is whether the real wiring learns better than the same neurons wired at random. That control is the next experiment.

Built with the FlyWire connectome (Dorkenwald et al. 2024; Schlegel et al. 2024) and the whole-brain spiking model of Shiu et al. 2024. Trained with surrogate-gradient backpropagation through time. Code: AIBRAIN — ask me for it.
